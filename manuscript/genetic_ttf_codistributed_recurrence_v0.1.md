# Shared landscape opportunity does not make phylogeographic structure portable across insect lineages

**Empirical manuscript v0.1 — codistributed recurrence**

Author list: TBD

Target journal: deferred until scope and presentation are finalized.

## Abstract

Comparative phylogeography is built on the expectation that species exposed to the same landscape may acquire concordant spatial genetic structure. Yet most evidence for such concordance is retrospective: phylogeographies are compared after the focal lineages and their breaks are already known. This leaves a stronger ecological question unresolved. If two lineages share more of the same geography, does spatial genetic information learned from one become more useful for predicting the other?

We tested this prospectively in a fresh insect panel derived from an authenticated phylogatR COI/COX1 archive. Geographic co-opportunity between each source-target pair was defined before genetic response opening as the minimum of reciprocal 500-km edge-midpoint coverage. The confirmatory design retained 326 character-qualified species, comprising 166 source and 160 target lineages and 26,560 directed source-target pairs. Co-opportunity remained non-degenerate after exact source and target identity, sampling imbalance, same-order membership and centroid distance were controlled: its unique variance fraction was 0.198. Before nucleotide identity was opened, the exact survivor geometry passed lineage-private false-positive calibration and a frozen shared-place positive-control test (power 0.946, Wilson 95% lower 0.923).

In the single authorized empirical opening, post-IBD source-to-target transfer was highly heterogeneous across pairs (SD 0.313; range -0.924 to 0.941), but that heterogeneity did not increase with shared geographic opportunity. The primary co-opportunity coefficient was -0.00445, and the least-favourable private-world Monte Carlo envelope gave p = 0.969. Thus the prospectively frozen decision was a qualified null: greater shared sampled geography was not sufficient to make post-IBD phylogeographic structure more reusable across lineages.

This result rules out the simplest explanation for weak cross-lineage recurrence—namely that lineages merely failed to experience enough of the same places. It does not imply that geography or physical barriers are unimportant. Instead, it suggests that the effective phylogeographic meaning of place is strongly lineage conditioned: the same landscape can be translated into different genetic structure by different organisms. Future mechanism tests should therefore focus on lineage-by-place interactions such as dispersal, habitat dependence and historical range dynamics rather than search for one universal barrier map.

## Introduction

Comparative phylogeography asks how geography becomes evolutionary history. Mountains, rivers, coastlines, climatic transitions, refugia and other landscape features can restrict movement repeatedly, creating the possibility that independently evolving species acquire spatially concordant genetic structure. Classical comparative studies therefore treat repeated breaks or shared regional histories among codistributed taxa as evidence for common geographic causes.

But a landscape feature is not a barrier in the abstract. Its effective permeability depends on the organism encountering it. A river can be a major discontinuity for one lineage and nearly irrelevant for another; a climatic transition can divide habitat specialists while leaving generalists connected; historical range shifts can cause lineages occupying the same present-day region to have encountered the landscape in different ways. Comparative phylogeography has increasingly recognized that discordance among codistributed taxa can therefore be biologically informative rather than mere noise.

Most comparative analyses, however, begin after the focal phylogeographies are already observed. They ask whether known breaks coincide, whether divergence histories overlap, or which candidate barriers align with mapped genetic discontinuities. These approaches are powerful for describing concordance, but they do not directly test whether information learned from one lineage generalizes to another lineage that was absent from discovery.

A genuinely reusable geographic component makes a prospective prediction. If place itself contributes a sufficiently lineage-general component to population differentiation, then source lineages should reveal spatial information that predicts unseen target lineages. More specifically, if weak transfer among arbitrary species is caused mainly by a failure to occupy the same places, then transfer should increase continuously as source and target lineages share more geographic opportunity.

That prediction is not trivial to test. Greater geographic overlap can mechanically improve the support of any spatial predictor even when every lineage has independent spatial structure. Ordinary isolation by distance can also create broad similarity without implying a repeated place-specific effect. We therefore define the biological response as post-IBD source-to-target transfer in excess of the transfer expected from lineage-private spatial worlds with the same realized sampling geometry. Geographic co-opportunity is measured response-blind, before nucleotide identity is opened.

The present study was motivated by a completed broad analysis of 211 species in which post-IBD spatial genetic structure was detectable within species but not detectably reusable across unseen lineages. The strongest ecological alternative was straightforward: perhaps many source-target pairs simply did not share enough geography for recurrence to be possible. We designed a fresh prospective insect experiment to test that explanation directly.

Our hypothesis was directional. If shared landscape opportunity conditions geographic recurrence, then source-target transfer beyond lineage-private expectations should increase with mutual co-opportunity. If the qualified design instead yields no positive relationship, then lack of shared geography is insufficient to explain broad non-transfer, shifting the problem toward lineage-dependent translation of place into genetic structure.

The study resolved to the second outcome.

## Results

### 1. Shared geographic opportunity remained a genuine relational predictor after controls

The fresh response-blind insect domain contained 762 species and was split deterministically into independent development and confirmatory panels of 381 species each. The confirmatory panel inherited 190 source and 191 target roles before any genetic response was opened.

Character-validity screening was then applied without revealing nucleotide identity. A species survived only when every unchanged frozen graph edge retained at least one cross-locality sequence pair meeting the frozen comparable-site requirement. This left 326 species: 166 sources and 160 targets, defining 26,560 directed source-target pairs.

The focal predictor, mutual geographic co-opportunity (G_{st}), was the minimum of reciprocal 500-km edge-midpoint coverage. Despite survivor filtering, (G_{st}) retained substantial independent information after exact source and target effects and frozen controls for edge-count imbalance, locality-count imbalance, same-order membership and centroid distance. Its total unique variance fraction was 0.1982.

Residual information was broadly distributed rather than dominated by a few endpoints. The maximum source signal share was 0.0169 and the maximum target signal share was 0.0189. Thus failure of the biological test could not be attributed simply to a degenerate or endpoint-concentrated geographic predictor.

### 2. The realized survivor geometry could control private-world false positives and detect the frozen shared-place signal

Before confirmatory nucleotide identity was opened, the exact survivor geometry was requalified using the same fixed source-to-target operator that would later be applied empirically.

Four lineage-private amplitude classes were evaluated independently. Their empirical rejection rates under the frozen least-favourable envelope were 0.048, 0.000, 0.004 and 0.046 for private amplitudes 0.5, 1, 2 and 3, respectively. The corresponding Wilson 95% upper bounds were 0.0704, 0.0076, 0.0145 and 0.0681, all below the frozen 0.10 ceiling.

The frozen shared-place positive control was detected in 473 of 500 worlds, giving power 0.946 and a Wilson 95% lower bound of 0.9226. The confirmatory survivor geometry therefore passed both false-positive control and positive-signal detectability before genetic response opening.

### 3. Source-to-target phylogeographic transfer was heterogeneous but unrelated to shared geographic opportunity

The single authorized empirical opening generated post-IBD source-to-target transfer scores for all 26,560 directed dyads.

Raw transfer skill varied substantially among lineage pairs. (T_{st}) ranged from -0.9235 to 0.9411, with standard deviation 0.3129. The mean was 0.00179 and the median was 0. Thus there was extensive pairwise heterogeneity to explain even though the average transfer across all dyads was near zero.

After subtracting each dyad's geometry-specific private-world expectation, the primary excess-transfer response (E_{st}) likewise remained broadly distributed (SD 0.3205; range -1.259 to 1.232).

However, greater mutual geographic co-opportunity did not predict greater excess transfer. The frozen primary coefficient was

[
eta_G = -0.004447.
]

The four component Monte Carlo p-values against the frozen private-reference distributions were 0.969, 0.777, 0.705 and 0.734. The least-favourable primary p-value was therefore 0.969, with the A = 0.5 private world providing the most conservative reference.

The prospectively frozen directional criterion (eta_G>0) with envelope (ple0.05) was not met. The result was classified as:

**CONFIRMATORY_QUALIFIED_NULL_SHARED_GEOGRAPHIC_OPPORTUNITY_INSUFFICIENT.**

The negative coefficient is not interpreted as evidence that shared geography reduces recurrence. The predeclared test was one-sided for a positive recurrence effect, and the descriptive two-way-cluster z-score (-1.07) has no confirmatory decision authority.

## Discussion

### Shared geography did not rescue cross-lineage recurrence

The central result is simple: lineages that shared more sampled geography did not show greater reuse of post-IBD spatial genetic structure.

This matters because it directly tests the most obvious explanation for weak cross-lineage transfer. If source and target species simply occupied different places, then broad non-transfer would say little about whether geography itself is reusable. Under that explanation, recurrence should strengthen as the amount of mutually sampled landscape increases.

It did not.

The result therefore narrows the ecological interpretation of broad non-transfer. Weak recurrence is not explained simply by lack of geographic co-opportunity.

### The same place need not have the same phylogeographic meaning

The qualified null should not be read as evidence that geography is unimportant. Each lineage can still be strongly affected by physical barriers, environmental transitions or historical spatial processes. What failed was the stronger proposition that the effect of place is sufficiently lineage invariant to be transported across species merely because they share more of that landscape.

This distinction reframes comparative phylogeographic discordance. A geographic feature has no single effective barrier value independent of organismal context. Its evolutionary effect can depend on dispersal, habitat dependence, demographic history, historical occupancy and other properties of the lineage interacting with it.

In that sense, the effective phylogeographic landscape is relational rather than purely geographic.

### Why this differs from retrospective concordance

Classical comparative phylogeography asks whether already-observed breaks or histories are spatially concordant. The present test asked whether spatial information learned from a source lineage predicts an excluded target, and whether that reuse increases continuously with response-blind geographic co-opportunity.

A retrospective concordance pattern can be scientifically real without being sufficiently regular to predict an unseen lineage. Conversely, successful out-of-lineage prediction would have provided direct evidence that some component of spatial differentiation was portable across lineages.

The observed null is therefore not merely a failure to find overlapping breaks. It is a failed prospective prediction about the reuse of place information.

### The result is informative because the design had both relation information and calibrated power

Null results in comparative datasets are often hard to interpret because sampling geometry may not contain enough overlap or because the estimator may be unable to detect the hypothesized effect. Neither explanation is sufficient here.

The response-blind co-opportunity predictor retained nearly 20% unique variance after frozen nuisance controls. Its residual signal was broadly distributed across sources and targets. The exact survivor geometry controlled false positives across all four declared lineage-private worlds and detected the frozen shared-place positive control with 0.946 power.

The empirical result therefore represents a qualified absence of the predicted positive relation, not a simple absence of statistical information.

### Relation to the broad 211-species result

The earlier broad analysis found that post-IBD spatial genetic structure was not detectably transferable across unseen species. The codistributed experiment was designed as a fresh test of one specific explanation for that outcome: insufficient shared geographic opportunity.

Because the new result was obtained in an independent response-blind insect panel and under a separately qualified one-shot design, it does not reclassify or retune the completed broad result. Instead, it removes one plausible explanation for it.

Together the two results support a sharper statement:

> Spatial genetic structure can be real within lineages yet fail to become portable across lineages even when those lineages share more of the same sampled landscape.

### What remains unexplained

The empirical (T_{st}) distribution was broad, demonstrating substantial source-target heterogeneity. Geographic co-opportunity did not explain that heterogeneity, but the present study was not designed to identify which lineage-specific mechanisms do.

The next mechanistic hypotheses should concern lineage-by-place interactions: dispersal capacity, habitat dependence, historical range dynamics, demographic response, or other processes that alter effective landscape permeability. These hypotheses require genuinely fresh response data or independent external validation. The opened 326-species confirmatory response cannot now be mined to choose a favourable mechanism and then treated as confirmatory evidence.

### Limitations

First, the empirical genetic response is mitochondrial COI/COX1. Mitochondrial structure can differ from nuclear genomic structure because of marker-specific effective population size, inheritance and introgression. The result therefore concerns the tested mitochondrial phylogeographic signal and should not be assumed to generalize automatically to nuclear genomes.

Second, geographic co-opportunity is based on sampled localities and graph-edge midpoints. It measures mutual opportunity in the observed spatial design, not historical coexistence or exact temporal overlap.

Third, the primary geographic scale was frozen at 500 km. The result does not imply that no other spatial scale can show recurrence. Post-result scale search is intentionally not authorized.

Fourth, the panel is a broad opportunistically assembled insect dataset rather than a deliberately sampled single biogeographic region. This increases taxonomic scope but limits direct attribution to a named geological history or barrier system.

Finally, the qualified null does not show that no biologically coherent subset can share phylogeographic structure. It shows that greater shared geographic opportunity alone is insufficient as a general source-target predictor in the tested domain.

---

## Conclusion

Comparative phylogeography often starts from the intuition that lineages occupying the same landscapes should carry repeated geographic signatures. We tested the most direct form of that intuition prospectively: whether sharing more sampled landscape makes post-IBD spatial genetic structure learned from one insect lineage more reusable for another.

It did not.

The simplest explanation for weak cross-lineage recurrence is therefore insufficient. The same place can matter to many lineages without leaving the same portable genetic pattern in each of them.

The remaining biological problem is not whether geography matters, but **why different lineages translate the same geography into different evolutionary structure**.

Licensed headline:

> **Sharing the same landscape is not sufficient to make phylogeographic structure reusable across lineages.**

Licensed inference:

- simple geographic co-opportunity is insufficient to restore cross-lineage recurrence in the qualified insect domain;
- the completed broad non-transfer result cannot be explained simply by species occupying or sampling different places;
- the same sampled landscape need not imply the same effective phylogeographic barrier structure;
- future mechanism tests should target lineage-by-place interactions.

Not licensed:

- geography is unimportant;
- physical barriers do not matter;
- all phylogeographic structure is lineage-specific;
- geographic co-opportunity has a negative causal effect;
- no biologically coherent subset can share barriers;
- the result is universal across Animalia.
